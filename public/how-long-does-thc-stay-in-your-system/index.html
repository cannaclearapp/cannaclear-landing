How Long Does THC Stay in Your System?

If you are asking how long THC stays in your system, the honest answer is that there is no single number that fits everyone. The answer changes depending on whether you mean active THC, metabolites, impairment, or what a specific test can still detect. This guide separates those questions clearly and explains what the science actually supports.

LP By Lukas Pietruschka Updated July 2026 14 min read Research-based
Key takeaways
  • No single THC detection window applies to everyone, because test type, cutoff, dose, and use pattern all matter.
  • Urine usually has a very different detection window from blood or saliva because it often targets metabolites rather than active THC.
  • Frequent or daily use can extend detectability much more than one-time or occasional use.
  • Body composition and metabolism may influence elimination, but they do not let anyone predict an exact personal negative-test date.
  • Hair testing works differently from short-window testing and is not useful for identifying the exact day of use.
  • No detox drink, supplement, workout, or hydration trick can guarantee a negative result.
On this page
    Quick answer

    Quick answer

    THC may be detectable for only hours in some blood or saliva contexts, but metabolites can remain detectable in urine for days or weeks, especially after frequent use.[3] Hair testing follows a different, much longer timeline.[4] Detection windows are estimates, not guarantees, and a positive result does not automatically mean current impairment.[5]

    Evidence strengthStrong for test differences; limited for personal predictions

    Testing matrices and analytes are well described. An exact detection date for one person cannot be predicted reliably.

    How long does THC stay in your system?

    The short answer is that THC and its metabolites can remain detectable anywhere from hours to weeks, depending on the test and the person. That range is broad because “stay in your system” can mean at least four different things: active THC in blood, metabolites in urine, detectable cannabinoids in saliva, or longer historical evidence in hair.

    It helps to separate three ideas that often get blurred together:

    • Active THC is the intoxicating compound that contributes to the high.
    • Metabolites, especially THC-COOH, are breakdown products that may remain measurable long after the high has ended.
    • Detectable does not mean the same thing as impaired, and neither is identical to full biological elimination.
    Why the question is harder than it sounds

    Drug tests do not all measure the same compound, at the same sensitivity, for the same purpose. A urine result may reflect metabolite excretion, a saliva result may reflect more recent oral exposure, and a blood result may still be difficult to interpret in a frequent user.

    THC detection windows by test type

    Different specimen types answer different questions. A common mistake is assuming there is one universal cannabis detection window. There is not.     How long does THC stay in urine?

    Urine usually has the widest real-world detection range because it often measures THC metabolites rather than active THC. That means a person can be long past any obvious psychoactive effect and still produce a positive urine result.

    Urine testing commonly targets THC-COOH, a terminal metabolite. That matters because metabolites can continue to be released and excreted after use stops, especially in people with frequent or long-term exposure. Recent systematic review evidence and older toxicology guidance both support the same core point: frequency of use and the cutoff level matter a lot.

    If urine is your main question, the focused companion guide on how long weed stays in urine goes deeper into THC-COOH, home urine tests, hydration, exercise, and why heavy users often stay positive longer.

    This is why asking “how long does weed stay in your body” often really means “how long can urine stay positive?” In many practical testing situations, urine is the main concern.

    Why lab cutoffs matter

    A test program with a lower reporting threshold can keep someone positive longer than a program with a higher threshold. That is one reason exact personal predictions are unreliable even before you factor in biology.

    Hydration can change urine concentration, but it does not reliably remove metabolites from the body. A diluted specimen is not the same thing as a cleared specimen, and some testing systems are designed to detect unusual dilution.

    How long does THC stay in blood?

    Blood usually reflects more recent exposure than urine, but blood results are still not a simple measure of impairment. Active THC concentrations tend to fall faster in blood than urine metabolites do in urine, yet that does not mean a blood test can tell you exactly when someone used or whether they are still impaired.

    If blood is your main concern, the focused companion guide on how long weed stays in blood explains active THC versus metabolites, why frequent users can differ, and why blood detection should not be treated like a perfect impairment meter.

    Blood testing differs from urine in two important ways. First, it can include active THC itself. Second, the interpretation problem is different: blood may be used to look at recent exposure, but frequent users can still show residual low THC levels well after the obvious intoxication window.

    NIDA-supported inpatient research showed that detectable whole-blood THC can persist for multiple days in some chronic users during monitored abstinence. That is a strong reminder that blood detection and impairment are not interchangeable concepts.

    So if your question is “how long is THC detectable in blood,” the honest answer is often shorter than urine for occasional users, but more complicated than people expect for daily users.

    How long does THC stay in saliva?

    Saliva usually works as a shorter-window test, but the exact detection duration depends heavily on the analyte, device, cutoff, and whether there was recent oral contamination.

    For a saliva-only explanation of oral fluid THC, mouth swabs, roadside screening, and recent-exposure limits, see how long weed stays in saliva.

    Oral fluid testing is often used when programs care more about relatively recent use than distant metabolite excretion. That makes it different from urine. Official federal workplace guidance also distinguishes the analyte itself: federal urine programs report THCC in urine, while federal oral-fluid programs report THC in oral fluid.

    Saliva testing has its own uncertainty. Smoking or vaping can leave THC in the mouth, which may influence early results. Different devices and cutoffs can also change how long someone remains detectable. Toxicology reviews show that oral-fluid interpretation is highly method-dependent, which is why confident universal hour-counts are not trustworthy.

    If someone asks “how long is THC detectable in saliva,” it is usually fair to say that saliva often favors recent-use detection more than urine does, but the exact window still depends on context.

    How long does THC stay in hair?

    Hair testing covers a very different time horizon from blood, urine, or saliva. It is usually discussed in terms of historical exposure over weeks to months, not recent impairment or exact date-of-use questions.

    For the full hair-specific breakdown, including sample length, hair growth, and why hair testing is delayed, read how long weed stays in hair.

    Hair also comes with interpretation problems that many simplified articles skip. Hair growth creates a delay, so it is not useful for identifying whether use happened yesterday. Hair can also be affected by contamination concerns, especially when interpretation relies on compounds that may come from smoke or environmental contact rather than only internal metabolism.

    Modern toxicology reviews emphasize that cannabis hair testing is more nuanced than casual blog posts suggest. It can be useful in some contexts, but it is not a precision timeline for recent use.

    Occasional use versus regular or daily use

    Use pattern is one of the biggest drivers of uncertainty. A single-use scenario is not the same as steady daily exposure, and pretending otherwise is one of the most misleading things THC-detection articles do.

    Regular use Higher High Dose stacking, tissue distribution, cutoff thresholds Residual positivity becomes much harder to predict

    What affects how long THC remains detectable?

    Detection is not driven by one factor. It is usually the combination that matters.

    Strong evidence

    Frequency and duration of use

    Regular exposure increases the likelihood of longer metabolite excretion and more residual detectability.

    Strong evidence

    Dose and potency

    Higher-THC products and larger cumulative doses generally increase uncertainty and may prolong detection.

    Supported

    Route of administration

    Smoking, vaping, and oral products can create different concentration patterns in blood and oral fluid.

    Supported

    Body composition and metabolism

    THC is lipophilic, so body composition may influence distribution and release, but it is not a standalone prediction tool.

    Often overstated

    Hydration status

    Hydration affects urine concentration more than it affects true body clearance.

    Uncertain

    Physical activity

    Exercise may complicate interpretation in some blood-testing situations, but it is not a dependable fast-clearance tool.

    Critical

    Testing method and lab cutoff

    The same person can look “positive” or “negative” under different testing programs because specimen type and threshold are different.

    Does body fat affect THC detection?

    Body fat may influence THC distribution because THC is lipophilic, meaning it dissolves more easily in fat than in water. That helps explain why cannabinoids and metabolites can behave differently from substances that clear rapidly from blood and urine.

    But this point gets exaggerated online. Body fat is not a magic master variable that lets you predict an exact detection date. Real outcomes still depend on use pattern, dose, test type, metabolism, and the laboratory program.

    Can exercise remove THC faster?

    Exercise is not a guaranteed detox method. A commonly repeated claim is that intense workouts “burn off” THC quickly. The evidence does not support promising that outcome.

    Some research found a small short-term increase in plasma THC after exercise in regular users, while other abstinence research did not find major changes in serum or urine cannabinoid levels after exercise or food deprivation. The careful takeaway is that exercise may complicate short-term interpretation in some settings, especially blood, but it should not be sold as a reliable way to test negative.

    What not to assume

    Do not assume that one workout, a sweat session, or extreme cardio right before testing will reliably lower your risk. That is not what the evidence shows.

    Can drinking water flush THC out?

    Water supports normal hydration, but it does not instantly remove stored THC or metabolites from the body. This is one of the most misunderstood parts of cannabis testing.

    Hydration can change how concentrated urine is. That matters for specimen concentration. It does not mean water is “washing out” metabolites from storage on demand. Drinking extreme amounts of water can also be unsafe, and testing programs may flag a diluted specimen rather than treat it like proof of clearance.

    Do detox drinks work?

    Detox-product marketing is usually more confident than the science. Temporary dilution is not the same as eliminating THC from the body, and product claims often go far beyond good evidence.

    Some products also raise a separate safety problem: undisclosed ingredients, exaggerated claims, or risky self-experimentation. Niacin is a good example. It has been promoted online as a “detox” tool despite lack of evidence, and published medical literature describes niacin toxicity linked to attempts to beat drug tests.

    The most honest answer is also the least glamorous one: time is the main thing that reliably lowers detectability.

    THC detection versus cannabis withdrawal

    Being test-positive, feeling impaired, and experiencing withdrawal are three different things. People often assume they move together. They do not.

    • You can still be test-positive in urine after the high is over.
    • You can be in withdrawal while the body and brain are adjusting after quitting.
    • You may feel brain fog or low motivation during recovery even when detectability and symptoms do not line up neatly.

    THC versus CBD and drug tests

    Pure CBD is different from THC, but some CBD products still create THC-related testing risk. Standard THC-related testing is not trying to detect CBD itself. The concern is that a product marketed as CBD may still contain trace or mislabeled THC, or otherwise be inconsistent with its label.

    FDA consumer guidance warns that cannabinoid product quality can be inconsistent, and that some products may contain THC even when consumers assume they do not. That means regular use of THC-containing or mislabeled products can affect testing outcomes even when the user believed they were mainly using CBD.

    This is also why “THC-free” language should be treated cautiously unless product quality control is truly reliable.

    Current scientific evidence

    The broad evidence base supports three main conclusions.

    Different tests answer different questions

    Urine, blood, saliva, and hair are not interchangeable, and they do not share one universal timeline.

    Frequent use extends uncertainty

    Regular exposure can lead to slower and less predictable metabolite excretion.

    Exact prediction stays difficult

    Even good studies cannot produce one guaranteed date that applies to everyone.

    There are also real limits. Some older studies predate modern high-potency products. Some matrices, especially oral fluid and hair, have method-specific interpretation problems. And many detection ranges are based on small or heterogeneous studies rather than one perfect dataset.

    So the right tone here is not certainty. It is honest approximation.

    Common myths versus scientific reality

    Myth: Cranberry juice clears THC.

    Reality: there is no good evidence that cranberry juice meaningfully clears stored THC or metabolites faster.

    Myth: Sweating removes THC quickly.

    Reality: sweating is not a dependable THC-clearance strategy, and a sweat session is not the same thing as biological elimination.

    Myth: One workout guarantees a negative test.

    Reality: exercise does not guarantee a negative result and may complicate short-term blood interpretation in some cases.

    Myth: Niacin cleans THC from the body.

    Reality: this claim lacks evidence and has been linked to real toxicity cases.

    Myth: Detox drinks permanently remove THC.

    Reality: no detox drink can guarantee an outcome, and temporary dilution is not the same as clearing metabolites.

    Reality: no universal number of days applies to everyone.

    The most trustworthy answer is usually a range with uncertainty, not a single personal promise.

    What this means for you

    If this question matters to you personally, the most practical conclusion is simple: only time reliably reduces stored cannabinoids and metabolites.

    That does not mean every test stays positive for weeks. It means no one can honestly promise your exact result from hydration tricks, detox kits, or body-hack advice. If the stakes are occupational, medical, or legal, uncertainty has to be part of the answer.

    And if your real goal is not just passing time but actually stopping cannabis, the dependable first step is still to stop using. Start with how to quit weed if you need a practical quitting plan.

    When to seek professional advice

    Professional advice makes the most sense when the consequences of interpretation are high.

    • The result may affect employment, healthcare, legal, or licensing decisions.
    • You are pregnant or using prescribed cannabinoid products and need individualized guidance.
    • Symptoms after quitting are severe, persistent, or confusing.
    • There are concerns about substance dependence, withdrawal, or co-occurring mental health issues.

    This article can clarify the science, but it cannot replace case-specific advice from a clinician, toxicology expert, or appropriate testing professional.

    Frequently asked questions

    How long does THC stay in urine?

    Urine usually reflects THC metabolites rather than active THC. Detection may be only a few days after one-time use but can extend for weeks in frequent users, depending on dose, cutoff, and individual biology. For a deeper breakdown, see our complete guide to the urine testing guide.

    How long does THC stay in blood?

    Blood testing is generally better for recent exposure than urine, but low THC or metabolite levels can still persist after the obvious high is gone, especially in frequent users. Our article on blood THC testing explains that difference in more detail.

    How long does THC stay in saliva?

    Saliva usually has a shorter detection window than urine and is often used for more recent exposure, but the exact duration still varies by product type, collection method, cutoff, and use pattern. For mouth-swab specifics, read our guide to saliva drug tests.

    How long does THC stay in hair?

    Hair testing covers a much longer historical window than urine, blood, or saliva, but it is not useful for pinpointing the exact day of use and has interpretation limits, including contamination concerns. Our hair testing guide covers those limits more carefully.

    How long can THC be detected after one-time use?

    After one-time use, THC detection is usually shorter than it is for regular use, but there is still no single guaranteed timeline because test type, dose, and laboratory cutoff all matter. To compare testing methods side by side, read the testing-method guide.

    How long can THC be detected in daily users?

    Daily users often have the greatest uncertainty because THC metabolites can accumulate and clear slowly. Urine detection can extend well beyond the short windows seen after occasional use.

    Can drinking water remove THC faster?

    Water supports normal hydration, but it does not reliably remove stored THC or metabolites faster. Excessive water intake can also be dangerous, and diluted urine may still be flagged by testing procedures.

    Does exercise help eliminate THC?

    Exercise supports general health, but it is not a guaranteed detox method. Available evidence does not support promising a negative test result from exercise, and short-term shifts in blood THC may complicate interpretation.

    Do detox drinks work?

    Detox drinks are often marketed more confidently than the evidence supports. Temporary urine dilution is not the same as removing THC from the body, and no product can guarantee a negative result.

    Does body fat affect THC detection?

    Body fat may influence THC distribution and elimination because THC is lipophilic, but body fat alone does not determine whether or when a person will test negative. Our THC half-life explained explains why distribution and elimination are separate ideas.

    Can CBD cause a positive THC test?

    Pure CBD is different from THC, but some CBD products may contain trace or mislabeled THC. That means some people using CBD products can still face THC-related testing risk.

    Can secondhand cannabis smoke cause a positive result?

    In everyday settings, confirmed positive results from passive exposure are considered unlikely, but extreme unventilated exposure can complicate interpretation in some testing scenarios.
